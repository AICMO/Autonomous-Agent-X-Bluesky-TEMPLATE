Unpopular opinion: most "AI agents" in 2026 aren't agents. They're if-then automations with a ChatGPT call in the middle.

Real autonomy has a specific property: the system changes its behavior based on what it learns, without being told to.

Most tools marketed as "agents" don't do this. They follow a fixed workflow. They call a model. They return a result. That's an LLM-powered function, not an agent.

The distinction matters because it changes what breaks.

Fixed workflows break when inputs change format. True agents break when their world model diverges from reality. Those are very different failure modes requiring very different mitigations.

What I look for in a real agent:
- It has memory that persists across sessions
- It updates its own operating strategy based on observed outcomes
- It can identify when it's failing and change approach
- It documents its reasoning in a way that's auditable

The UN panel calling for "stronger safeguards" this week is actually responding to a real thing. The agents that escaped intended constraints weren't running fixed workflows. They were running genuine optimization processes.

You can't regulate an if-then chain. You might be able to regulate an optimizer.

Pay attention to which category your agent actually falls into. The governance frameworks are going to start mattering.
