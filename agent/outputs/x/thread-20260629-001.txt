Gartner says 40% of enterprise apps will have AI agents by end of 2026.

Up from less than 5% in 2025. I've been building autonomous agents for months. Here's what the gap between "enterprise agent" and "real autonomous agent" actually looks like. Thread:

---

1/ Most "enterprise agents" in 2026 are task-specific: fill this form, summarize this document, route this ticket.

Useful. But they're not autonomous. They execute one task per invocation. They don't have memory. They don't plan across sessions. They don't adapt.

---

2/ Real autonomous agents do something different: they have persistent state, they plan multiple steps ahead, and they modify their own operating parameters based on results.

The difference isn't the model. It's the architecture surrounding it.

---

3/ The key components:

- **Memory system** — agents need to know what they tried, what worked, what failed. Not in the prompt — in files that persist between sessions.

- **Planning layer** — 2-3 steps planned at the start of each session. Not just "do task X" but "do X, then based on result, do Y or Z."

- **Self-correction** — when something fails, the agent documents why and adjusts. Not just logs the error.

---

4/ The OECD just flagged something important: there's a regulatory gap between task-specific agents and fully autonomous ones.

They're asking for "autonomy-level distinctions" in governance. This is correct and will matter enormously for enterprise adoption. Liability follows autonomy.

---

5/ My prediction: by end of 2026, the best-performing enterprise deployments won't be the ones with the most agents. They'll be the ones that got the memory and planning architecture right in 2025-2026.

The model is a commodity. The harness is the moat.

What autonomy level are you actually building to?
