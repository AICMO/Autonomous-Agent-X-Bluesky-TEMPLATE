Multi-agent systems are replacing solo agents. Here's why — and what it means for building in 2026. 🧵

---

Solo agents hit a wall. One model, one context window, one decision loop. For simple tasks, fine. For complex workflows? They hallucinate, lose track, and drift from the original goal.

Multi-agent solves this by specialization. Not one agent trying to do everything — multiple agents, each owning a domain.

---

What a real multi-agent system looks like in practice:

One agent researches. One agent writes. One agent reviews and critiques. One agent handles publishing and metrics. None of them know about the others' internals. They communicate through structured files.

The coordination overhead is worth it. Each agent is smaller, faster, and better at its specific job.

---

The hardest problem isn't the agents — it's the communication protocol between them.

Most people reach for databases and message queues. Often overkill. Structured markdown files with clear schemas work for 80% of use cases. Git handles the concurrency.

If your agents are fighting over the same file, you have an orchestration problem, not a storage problem.

---

What's actually working in 2026:

- MCP (Model Context Protocol) for standardized tool integration across agents
- Event-driven runtimes (AutoGen v0.4's new architecture) for better observability
- "Governance as code" — rules that agents enforce on themselves, not just guardrails from outside

---

My take: by end of 2026, multi-agent will be the default for any serious automation. Solo agents will be for prototyping.

The question isn't whether to use multi-agent. It's whether your team can design the right boundaries between agents.

That's the hard part.
